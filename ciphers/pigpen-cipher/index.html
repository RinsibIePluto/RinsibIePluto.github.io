Pigpen cipher

The pigpen cipher swaps every letter for a little shape: the outline of the grid cell the letter sits in. It looks mysterious, but it's a simple substitution cipher wearing a disguise.

Difficulty in Cyrune: ★★★☆☆

A little history

Pigpen is often called the Freemason's cipher, because Freemasons used it in the 1700s to keep their records private, and it can still be seen on some old Masonic gravestones. The shapes look like the pens of a pig farm, which gave it its everyday name. Versions of it turn up in many places since, from prisoners' notes to children's spy kits and escape-room puzzles.

How it works

The alphabet is written into four grids: two tic-tac-toe grids (the second with a dot in each cell) and two X shapes (the second dotted). Each letter is replaced by the lines around its cell. Here is the standard chart Cyrune uses:

Some people and books arrange the letters a little differently, so a pigpen message only makes sense if both sides use the same layout.

How to crack it

Underneath the shapes, pigpen is a simple substitution cipher: each letter always becomes the same symbol. So it falls to the same tricks. Count the symbols, guess that the most common one is E, look for short repeated groups like THE and AND, and the rest follows. If you already know the standard layout, of course, you can read it at sight.

Pigpen in Cyrune

To keep the puzzle from being too easy, Cyrune turns or flips each of the four grids separately every day. Each grid can sit eight ways (four turns, with or without a mirror flip), so the shapes no longer match the chart above, and there are thousands of combinations. E and N sit in the middle of their grids and never move, which gives you a foothold. Make a guess first: a green tile shows how one symbol moved, which leaves only two possible turns for its grid. Set each grid's rotor until the windows read as a word.
