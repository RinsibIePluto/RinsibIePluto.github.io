Shift + scramble

Shift and scramble stacks two simple ciphers: first every letter is shifted along the alphabet, then the letters are shuffled into a new order. Each step is easy to break alone. Together they hide each other's clues.

Difficulty in Cyrune: ★★★☆☆

Why combine ciphers?

A substitution cipher, like the shift, changes letters but keeps their order. A transposition cipher, like the rail fence, keeps the letters but changes their order. Each leaves a different trail: substitution keeps the shape of words, transposition keeps the letter counts. Doing one after the other removes both trails at once.

In 1949 the mathematician Claude Shannon described this idea as confusion (hiding the link between key and ciphertext) and diffusion (spreading each letter's influence around). Modern ciphers that protect your bank and messages still repeat rounds of substitution and shuffling, just millions of times faster.

How it works

  1. Shift every letter by the key, as in the Caesar cipher.
  2. Rearrange the shifted letters into a secret order.

How to crack it

Attack the shift first, because it has only 25 keys. For each possible shift, the lamps turn into a set of letters; the right shift is the one whose letters can be rearranged into a word. Then the puzzle becomes an anagram. The shuffle never changes which letters there are, so once the shift is right, you're unscrambling, not decrypting.

Shift and scramble in Cyrune

Turn the rotor to try each shift and watch the decoded letters; when they look like they could make a word, use your guesses to find the order. Green tiles tell you where a letter really belongs, and yellow tiles tell you it's in the word but somewhere else.
