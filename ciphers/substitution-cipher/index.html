Substitution cipher

A substitution cipher swaps every letter for another using a scrambled alphabet. There are about 400 million million million million possible alphabets, far too many to try. Yet a ninth-century scholar showed how to break it with nothing more than counting.

Difficulty in Cyrune: ★★★★☆

A little history

Substitution ciphers were the standard way to keep secrets for well over a thousand years. Around the 800s, the Arab scholar al-Kindi wrote the first known description of frequency analysis: in any language, some letters are far more common than others, and the same is true of their substitutes. In 1586, Mary, Queen of Scots trusted a substitution cipher to plot against Elizabeth I. Elizabeth's codebreaker Thomas Phelippes broke it by counting letters, and the decrypted letters led to Mary's execution.

How it works

Write out any rearrangement of the alphabet. That's the key. Each message letter is swapped for the letter in the same position of the key alphabet.

The number of possible keys is 26 × 25 × 24 × … × 1, written 26! (26 factorial): about 4 × 10²⁶. Even checking a billion keys a second would take billions of years.

How to crack it

Don't search keys; read patterns. In English, E, T, A and O are the most common letters, TH and HE the most common pairs, and THE the most common word. Count the cipher letters, match the most common ones, and look for words with telltale shapes: a three-letter word that appears often is probably THE, and a word like XYZZYX with repeated letters in those places can only be a few words. Each letter you fix makes the next guess easier.
